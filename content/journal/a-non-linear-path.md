Looking back at the experiences that have shaped my life thus far, I find it difficult to identify a single direction that connects them all. I began by working in public service at the New York State Senate, later entered the medical field as an anesthesia technician at Albany Medical Center, explored scientific research at the RNA Institute, and eventually found myself co-founding a technology startup while pursuing my undergraduate education at Tufts University.

At first glance, these experiences may appear disconnected, perhaps even indicative of someone who has struggled to settle on a particular ambition. For a long time, I viewed them as separate chapters, each representing a different interest or possible future. However, I have come to realize that the value of these experiences lies not necessarily in their relationship to a particular career, but in the lessons they taught me about discipline, responsibility, perseverance, and the people whose lives our work ultimately affects.

I have learned that personal growth rarely follows a perfectly defined trajectory. Sometimes, the experiences that appear least relevant to our immediate goals become the ones that prepare us most meaningfully for what comes next.

## The Senate: Learning the Value of Responsibility

My experience at the New York State Senate introduced me to the realities of working within a public institution, where individual responsibilities contribute to something much larger than oneself. As a production intern, I helped prepare printed communications and materials distributed by legislative offices to constituents throughout New York State. Although much of my work took place behind the scenes, it required precision, coordination, and an understanding that even seemingly routine tasks carried consequences beyond the office.

One of the most valuable lessons I learned was the importance of working effectively under deadlines. In a legislative environment, priorities can shift quickly, multiple requests may arrive simultaneously, and the expectation is that the work will be completed regardless of the circumstances. I gradually came to understand that professionalism is not simply about producing excellent work when conditions are ideal, but about maintaining reliability when time and resources are limited.

This experience fundamentally changed how I approach execution. I learned to distinguish between what genuinely requires perfection and what simply needs to be completed thoughtfully and on time. Today, as I navigate the uncertainties of entrepreneurship, that distinction remains particularly valuable. An idea, regardless of how promising it may be, cannot create meaningful change unless someone takes responsibility for turning it into reality.

## The Operating Room: Learning to Remain Calm Under Pressure

At seventeen, I began working as an anesthesia technician at Albany Medical Center, an experience that exposed me to a level of responsibility I had never encountered before. My role involved preparing anesthesia equipment, maintaining operating room readiness, and assisting anesthesia providers during surgical procedures and emergencies. I had the opportunity to work alongside medical professionals in situations where clear communication, preparation, and attention to detail were essential to patient safety.

The operating room taught me that remaining calm under pressure is not simply a personality trait, but a discipline developed through preparation and experience. During emergencies, there is little room for unnecessary confusion or hesitation. Every person has a responsibility, every instruction serves a purpose, and the effectiveness of the entire team depends on how well its members communicate and coordinate their actions.

One practice that particularly influenced me was closed-loop communication, in which instructions are acknowledged and repeated back to confirm that they have been understood. While seemingly simple, this approach reduces uncertainty and establishes accountability in environments where miscommunication can have serious consequences.

Although the challenges of running a startup are fundamentally different from those encountered in an operating room, I find myself drawing upon these lessons regularly. Building a company requires people to make decisions with incomplete information, navigate unexpected difficulties, and trust one another to fulfill their responsibilities. I have learned that when circumstances become uncertain, the most productive response is rarely panic. Instead, it is to communicate clearly, identify what matters most, and approach the problem with deliberate composure.

More importantly, working in healthcare reminded me that behind every professional responsibility is a human being whose well-being may depend on the quality of our work. That perspective continues to influence how I think about leadership and the obligations that accompany it.

## The Research Laboratory: Learning Patience and Intellectual Humility

My interest in understanding the human brain eventually led me to the RNA Institute, where I explored research involving spinocerebellar ataxia, a group of neurodegenerative disorders that affect movement and coordination. Through computational approaches to biological research, I gained exposure to the use of R, Linux-based tools, and biological datasets to investigate complex scientific questions.

Unlike environments where success is often measured through immediate results, scientific research requires a willingness to confront uncertainty over extended periods. A hypothesis that initially appears promising may fail to produce meaningful findings, while hours of analysis may reveal little more than the need to reconsider an earlier assumption.

Initially, I found this process frustrating. I was accustomed to approaching problems with the expectation that sustained effort would eventually produce a recognizable solution. Research challenged that assumption by showing me that effort and progress are not always immediately proportional. Sometimes, meaningful advancement begins with recognizing that an existing approach is incorrect.

Over time, I began to appreciate the intellectual humility that scientific inquiry demands. Good research requires the ability to question one's assumptions, acknowledge limitations, and remain open to conclusions that differ from what one initially hoped to discover.

I see a remarkable similarity between this process and entrepreneurship. Building a product involves forming hypotheses about what people need, testing those assumptions, and accepting that many ideas will not work as intended. The discipline of research taught me to view unsuccessful experiments not as personal failures, but as opportunities to improve my understanding of a problem.

## Entrepreneurship: Bringing the Lessons Together

When I eventually began building Briefly, an audio discovery platform designed to help people discover meaningful moments from podcasts, I initially regarded entrepreneurship as a significant departure from my previous experiences. Unlike medicine, public service, or scientific research, founding a company did not offer an established structure through which I could measure my progress or determine what steps to take next.

Yet, the longer I worked on the idea, the more I recognized how much my earlier experiences had prepared me for this unfamiliar environment.

The Senate taught me the importance of execution and accountability. The operating room taught me how to communicate effectively and remain composed when circumstances become difficult. Scientific research taught me patience, intellectual curiosity, and the importance of questioning assumptions. Together, these lessons provided a foundation that I might never have developed had I confined myself to a single discipline.

Entrepreneurship has also helped me recognize a common thread connecting my interests. Whether I was supporting public institutions, contributing to patient care, studying neurological disorders, or building a technology product, I was ultimately drawn to work that could improve the experiences and opportunities available to others.

Although the methods differed, the underlying motivation remained remarkably consistent.

## Being First: The Responsibility of Opportunity

As a first-generation college student, my educational journey has carried a particular significance. Pursuing higher education meant navigating an unfamiliar system, one filled with expectations, procedures, and opportunities that were not always immediately apparent to me or my family.

The college admissions process, in particular, required more than academic preparation. It demanded an understanding of how to present years of personal experiences, evaluate financial aid opportunities, and make consequential decisions about my future. I came to recognize that access to information and guidance can be just as important as individual ability, especially for students whose families have limited familiarity with the American higher education system.

Becoming a QuestBridge National College Match Finalist and eventually attending Tufts University represented important milestones in that journey. However, these experiences also made me increasingly conscious of the many capable students who face similar circumstances without sufficient access to mentorship or reliable guidance.

I believe that opportunity carries a responsibility to make the path more accessible for those who follow. For this reason, I offer free college admissions guidance to first-generation and low-income students who may benefit from additional support. Whether that involves reviewing an application, discussing a personal statement, or helping someone better understand the admissions process, I hope to share what I have learned in a way that makes an otherwise intimidating experience more manageable.

Students interested in receiving assistance can complete the brief survey available on my [Services page](/services#admissions). Although I cannot promise any particular admissions outcome, I can offer my time, perspective, and a genuine commitment to helping others navigate a process I once had to learn myself.

## What ties it together

Outside of work and class I train Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, cook, and practice languages. Jiu-Jitsu keeps teaching me the same lesson the rest of this path did: you improve by showing up and being a beginner again, over and over.

The line through all of it is curiosity about how people think, and a wish to build things that are good for them.

## Finding Meaning in an Unconventional Journey

I used to believe that having a clear sense of direction meant identifying a particular profession and pursuing it without deviation. Over time, however, my experiences have challenged that understanding. I have come to appreciate that uncertainty is not necessarily evidence of a lack of ambition, just as changing direction does not invalidate the effort invested in an earlier pursuit.

Every environment I have entered has expanded my understanding of what meaningful work can look like. Public service introduced me to institutional responsibility, medicine revealed the importance of human dignity and composure, research taught me intellectual humility, and entrepreneurship continues to challenge me to translate ideas into something useful for others.

I remain uncertain about many aspects of my future, and I suspect that my interests and ambitions will continue to evolve. Nevertheless, I am increasingly comfortable with the possibility that my life may never resemble the straightforward path.

What matters to me now is not whether every experience fits neatly into a predetermined plan, but whether each experience helps me become more capable, more thoughtful, and better prepared to contribute something meaningful to the world.

**Perhaps the purpose of an unconventional journey is not to arrive at a destination we anticipated, but to become someone capable of recognizing opportunities we could never have imagined at the beginning.**
