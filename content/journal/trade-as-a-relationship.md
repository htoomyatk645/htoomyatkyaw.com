I like studying how great operators build companies. The principles I keep coming back to, though, are older than any of them. I try to run Briefly by the Islamic ideals of commerce: honest dealing, fair value, and mutual consent.

These three ideas turn a transaction into a relationship. I'll take them one at a time.

## Honest dealing

Say what the product is, and say what it isn't. Briefly plays real clips from real episodes. It doesn't pretend an AI summary is the same as hearing the person speak, and it always gives you a way back to the full episode.

Honesty applies inside the team too. On a team of three, a hidden problem doesn't stay hidden for long, so it is better to name problems early.

## Fair value

Both sides should come out ahead. Listeners get the best moments of a show in a few minutes. Podcasters get their own voice in front of people who would not have found them otherwise. If only one side were winning, I would count that as a design flaw and not as a clever business model.

## Mutual consent

People should know what they are agreeing to. That rules out tricks: confusing sign-ups, buttons designed to be pressed by accident, and settings that are hard to undo.

## Why this is also good business

When both sides can trust what they are getting, customers stay and teams stay. The business earns something that is worth more than a good quarter, which is the trust of the people it serves.

> If it isn't good for everyone it touches, it isn't worth building.

I don't always get this right. But having the standard written down makes it much easier to notice when I'm about to fall short of it.
