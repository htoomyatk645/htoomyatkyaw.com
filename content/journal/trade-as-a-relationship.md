I've always been fascinated by how successful entrepreneurs build and sustain their companies. I enjoy studying their decisions, understanding their strategies, and learning what distinguishes businesses that endure from those that eventually disappear. Yet, the more I learn about entrepreneurship, the more I find myself returning to principles that have existed long before the modern idea of a startup. In Islam, commerce is not simply a means of generating wealth, but also a responsibility that demands integrity, fairness, and respect for those involved.

As I continue building Briefly, I want these principles to serve as the foundation of how we operate. In particular, three Islamic values have influenced the way I approach entrepreneurship: honest dealing, fair value, and mutual consent. Although each addresses a different aspect of commerce, together they reflect a broader philosophy that I believe every business should aspire to uphold. A successful transaction should not merely produce an exchange of money or services; it should establish a relationship built on trust and mutual benefit.

## Honest Dealing

Honesty in business begins with representing a product truthfully, acknowledging both what it can accomplish and where its limitations lie. It is easy for a startup to exaggerate its capabilities, especially when competing for attention in a crowded market. However, I believe that gaining someone's trust through an unrealistic promise ultimately costs more than the temporary advantage it provides.

At Briefly, this means being transparent about the experience we offer. Our platform allows listeners to discover meaningful moments from existing podcasts through short audio clips. We are not attempting to replace the depth of a complete conversation, nor do we believe that an AI-generated summary can fully capture the personality, emotion, and nuance of hearing someone speak. Instead, we want our clips to serve as an introduction to the original conversation, encouraging listeners to explore the full episode whenever their interest is sparked.

The same commitment to honesty should extend to the people building the company. In a small founding team, transparency is especially important because individual decisions have consequences for everyone involved. When challenges arise, I believe it is far more productive to acknowledge them openly than to conceal them out of fear or pride. A culture of honesty allows people to confront difficulties together rather than allowing misunderstandings to undermine their relationships.

## Fair Value

One of the most important lessons I have taken from Islamic commerce is that the pursuit of profit should not come at the expense of fairness. A business may be financially successful while still failing to create meaningful value for everyone participating in it. To me, genuine success requires more than profitability; it requires an exchange in which each party receives something worthwhile.

This idea is central to how I envision Briefly. For listeners, the platform offers an accessible way to discover interesting ideas and conversations without having to search through hours of content. For podcasters, it creates an opportunity to introduce their work to audiences who might otherwise never encounter it. Ideally, both groups benefit from participating in the platform, and the value created for one strengthens the experience of the other.

I also recognize that fairness is not something a company can establish once and assume will always remain intact. As a business grows, new incentives emerge, and decisions that increase revenue may not necessarily improve the experience of its users or creators. I want to remain conscious of these tensions and evaluate our decisions according to whether they preserve the balance of value between the people we serve. If Briefly eventually succeeds financially while leaving its listeners or podcasters worse off, I would consider that a failure of our original purpose.

## Mutual Consent

Mutual consent is another principle that shapes how I think about the relationship between a business and its customers. In Islamic commerce, an exchange should be entered into willingly, with both parties understanding the terms of their agreement. I believe this principle is particularly relevant to technology companies, where product design can influence people's decisions in ways they may not immediately recognize.

Digital platforms frequently rely on mechanisms intended to maximize engagement, encourage subscriptions, or make it difficult for users to leave. Although these practices may improve certain business metrics, they can also undermine the freedom and trust of the people using the product. I do not believe that a person's attention, time, or money should be obtained through confusion or manipulation.

For Briefly, respecting consent means designing an experience in which users understand the choices available to them and retain meaningful control over how they engage with the platform. Whether someone is signing up, changing a preference, or deciding to stop using the service, the process should be straightforward and transparent. I want people to return to Briefly because they genuinely find value in it, not because we have designed obstacles that make leaving inconvenient.

## Why These Principles Matter Beyond Ethics

Although these values originate from my religious understanding of commerce, I believe they also offer a practical foundation for building a sustainable company. Trust is difficult to earn and remarkably easy to lose. When customers consistently receive what they have been promised, when creators feel fairly treated, and when team members can rely on one another, a business develops relationships that cannot easily be replicated by competitors.

In an industry where success is often measured through growth, valuation, and market share, it can be tempting to treat ethical considerations as secondary to financial performance. I see them differently. I believe that the way a company achieves success matters just as much as the success itself. A business that grows by compromising the trust of the people it serves may enjoy short-term advantages, but it risks weakening the very relationships on which its future depends.

I am still learning what it means to build and operate a company, and I do not expect every decision to be simple. There will undoubtedly be moments when commercial incentives conflict with the principles I want to uphold. What matters to me is having a clear standard against which those decisions can be evaluated, even when following that standard requires taking a more difficult path.

Ultimately, I do not want Briefly to be remembered solely for the product we created or the financial success we may achieve. I want it to be an example of how a business can create value without compromising the dignity, trust, or interests of the people who make its existence possible.

**If building something successful requires abandoning the principles that made it worth building, then I believe we have misunderstood what success truly means.**
