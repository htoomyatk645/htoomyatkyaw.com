## Sterility Is Everything

*medicine · 2025-06-09*

I started in the ORs at seventeen, the youngest anesthesia technician there. It was my first weekend shift, and my trainer and I were asked to bring a pulse oximeter into an OR. We masked up, he handed me the device, and I walked in with him close behind me.

The anesthesia attending was on the far side of the drapes. My job sounded simple: get the device to them without disturbing the sterile field. The trouble was that I didn't really understand what that meant yet.

I moved as carefully as I could. Then I caught a look from one of the surgical technologists, the kind of look you don't need explained. I was an arm's length from the sterile field. My trainer quietly told me from behind that one more step and the team would have had to restart the case.

I still think about that moment. Until then I hadn't seen how much quiet work goes into keeping a patient safe during a procedure, or how much of it rests on every single person in the room, including the new kid holding a pulse ox.

Afterwards my trainer walked me through the etiquette of the OR, and I've kept every word of it. Never pick up something that was dropped on purpose. Always know where the sterile field is. And the rule I still repeat to myself: if it's blue and clear, don't touch it.

The same goes for helping providers gown and glove. Once they've scrubbed in they're sterile, and it's on us to keep them that way. One of them put it best, laughing as they said it: "Don't touch me, I'm sterile."

That was my first real lesson in the OR. Sterility is everything.

## My First Code

*medicine · 2025-06-23*

I was seventeen and the youngest anesthesia technician in the ORs, three weeks into my training, when we had a code. The patient was bleeding heavily from several places, the blood loss had taken their pulse, and CPR had started. My trainer, a seasoned anesthesia tech on his way to becoming a CRNA who had seen this many times, turned to me and told me to gown up, glove up, and grab a mask with a face shield.

I hesitated and said something like "I'm not ready for this." He said, more or less, "You're solid. It's now or never." So I gowned up. My hands were shaking so badly I could barely get the gloves on.

I was trembling to the point that I could feel my heart shaking. The only CPR I had ever done was on dummies, never in a real hospital code. Then my trainer told the nurses rotating on compressions to step aside, pointed at me, and said, "You're up next. Get ready."

I looked at him, nervous and confused, but there was no time to be afraid.

I stepped forward, still shaking, and started compressions on someone fighting for their life. Around me, residents, nurses, anesthesiologists and attendings moved in a blur: intubating, placing lines, calling for tools. My mind was racing and my body was running on adrenaline. In my head I was reciting every prayer I knew from the Quran.

Two minutes later I heard "Stop compressions" and stepped back. My gown and double gloves were stained with blood and sweat, and my face shield was fogged from my own breathing. My hands were still shaking. After the next round of compressions, the pulse came back.

That night my trainer told me, "Your form was perfect on those compressions." I was still trying to take in what I had just done and where I had just been.

I won't forget the thoughts I had that night, from changing out of my scrubs to the whole drive home with shaky hands and a racing heart. I went to bed asking myself hard questions. Do I really want to do medicine? What if I make a mistake?

But the next morning I caught myself smiling while I brushed my teeth. I couldn't hold it in. It wasn't the kind of happiness that comes from winning something. It was knowing I had been part of saving someone's life. I wasn't the most important person in that room. I understood maybe an eighth of the words being said. But I got to help a team of determined people do the most important thing there is, and that was enough to answer the questions I'd gone to sleep with. I found meaning and purpose in the work.

This job is unpredictable, intense and humbling. Every day at a Level 1 trauma center makes me value life more than I ever have, and it has shown me why it takes so many years of training to become someone who takes care of people.

## The Beginning of a Life

*medicine · 2025-07-14*

One afternoon in June, just as I got to work, an emergency was called over the intercom. My mentor told me to drop whatever I was holding and follow the anesthesia team.

We ran from the OR floor to the OB/GYN floor. People were moving faster than I had ever seen, faster even than in trauma cases. When we walked into the OB operating room, I realized what was about to happen. A new life was about to come into the world right in front of me.

The surgeons and nurses got ready for the delivery while anesthesia got consent from the mother, who chose not to be put fully asleep. It was the first time I saw an epidural. The anesthesia residents explained what it does while they placed it: it numbs the lower body and lets the mother stay awake. I'm still grateful they took the time to teach me in the middle of all that.

There wasn't much for an anesthesia tech to do in that moment, so mostly I watched. The team went ahead with a C-section, and within minutes the baby was out. I was standing a couple of feet away, and I could see the joy move through the room in the middle of all the chaos.

The newborn was handed to a nurse, who gently tapped the baby until the first cry came. Then she walked up to the drapes and said, "Say hi to Mommy." The mother got her first look before the baby was taken to the warmer.

While the surgical team finished up, I stepped outside to take in what I had just seen. Birth had always been a bit of a mystery to me. Now it was something real, something I could actually begin to understand.

I was still in high school then, and being in that room opened my eyes. Small, powerful moments like that one are shaping how I see healthcare: what it asks of people, what it struggles with, and why every role in the operating room matters.

It wasn't just another shift. It reminded me why I started down this road.

## My First Loss in the OR

*medicine · 2025-07-28*

The first time I saw someone die in the OR, it changed how I see medicine.

It was a normal shift until an alert went off: code blue. My mentor and I ran to the scene, but other techs had already arrived. Instead of heading back, my mentor told me to stay in case an extra pair of hands was needed.

The patient had just had surgery and was supposed to go home later that day. On the way back from the restroom, they fell and went into distress. In a blur, the team started CPR and called for the trauma room to be prepared. Off to the side, a family member was crying, "They were fine just a minute ago," their voice breaking. That moment showed me another side of medicine: the raw pain of families, and the love that keeps their hope alive.

I followed my mentor into the trauma room to learn how the anesthesia team gets ready for an emergency: checking the anesthesia machine, setting up the blood infusion equipment, arranging IVs, preparing the cell saver, and getting the lines ready for the providers.

Then the patient arrived and compressions started again. For almost an hour the surgical team worked with incredible speed and precision, everyone completely focused, moving faster than an F1 pit crew. Then the attending made the call. No one needed to say anything; everyone understood. The patient was sutured and covered with a white sheet, and the room went heavy and silent.

That's when it sank in. Some of the most skilled and dedicated people I have ever seen had been in that room, and it still wasn't enough to change the outcome. The air was thick with grief, and the silence said everything.

That day taught me that not every code ends with "we saved a life." Sometimes, despite everyone's effort, the story doesn't end the way we hope. Medicine isn't only about the wins. It's also about facing the moments when you can't win.

It reminded me how fragile life is, how quickly things can change, and how much it matters to fill our days with purpose and meaning, and with the pursuit of something bigger than ourselves, for us and for the people around us.

## A Heart at Seventeen

*medicine · 2025-08-18*

I was seventeen the first time I saw a human heart beating inside someone's chest.

I was working in the OR as an anesthesia tech when I got pulled into a CABG case, a coronary artery bypass graft. Surgeons call them "cabbage" cases. I had no idea what I was about to see.

The cardiac anesthesiologist I was working with that day noticed my wide eyes from across the drape. Instead of sending me out, she leaned over and said, "Come here. Let me show you something."

It turned into the most unforgettable anatomy lesson I've ever had.

First, the sternum. A saw cuts straight down the center of the chest. The surgeons set a retractor, a heavy metal instrument that looks like a door hinge, between the two halves, and as they turn the wheel the chest slowly opens, layer by layer. The Bovie, an electrocautery tool, burns through tissue with a smell I still can't quite describe. And then, through the pericardium, you see it.

The heart. Alive, and beating.

Over the drape, the cardiothoracic surgeon walked me through all of it: the right and left atria, the pulmonary arteries, and the aorta, which is far bigger up close than any diagram prepares you for. He pointed to the coronary arteries, the ones they were there to bypass, and explained how something so small, only a few millimeters wide, can decide whether someone goes home to their family.

I'm not pursuing medicine anymore. But that hour gave me something no classroom could have. It's why I keep chasing experiences where I learn by doing and by seeing.

I think back to that day whenever I need reminding how much is at stake in a single life, and that I once got to watch a small miracle up close.

## Leaving Medicine

*medicine · 2026-08-14*

Today I quit my job.

The reason is simple: I'm no longer pursuing medicine. There's a saying that the longer you stay on the wrong train, the more expensive the ride home, and I've come to believe it.

I became an anesthesia technician at seventeen, chasing the thrill of code blues alongside some of the best doctors in the country. I sweated through my scrub caps the first time I didn't know how to run a machine during a trauma. I left those ORs with a lasting sense of how fragile life is. It has been a full, memorable two years.

I started university with my mind made up: I'd be pre-med forever. But reality has a way of showing up during a 12-hour shift or an overnight. I spent a lot of time asking myself whether I wanted to walk into a hospital before sunrise and leave after sunset for the rest of my life. And the thrill the job once gave me faded a little more with every day I spent there.

I like uncertainty. My life has never been a straight road, and, to some people's surprise, I'm not as worried about stability as they are. I don't know exactly what's next as I move from medicine into a new industry. Honestly, that's the best part. It will strengthen my faith in God and push me to keep sharpening myself.

I'll be turning nineteen soon, and this feels like the beginning. I came up with the idea for Briefly in my dorm room, and seeing the actual product our team built on my phone still blows my mind. The idea was too good for me to let go of. It hasn't been easy, between supporting my family as a first-gen student, paying for the things I love like BJJ and Quranic studies, and working full time while building Briefly with my co-founders.

To everyone I worked with at Albany Medical Center who made my time there enjoyable and taught me so much: I'm forever grateful. I'll carry the lessons and the stories with me.

It's been an especially busy summer, and it only gets busier from here. I love it.

## Notes from Muslims in Business

*business-ethics*

Assalamu alaikum (peace be upon you).

Yesterday I attended the first Muslims in Business Conference at Harvard Business School with Suleiman Abuaqel. It was the largest gathering HBS has on record: thousands of Muslim entrepreneurs, students, and leaders from business and startups. I met people who want to solve modern problems while building Islamic principles and values into what they make, and it felt good to be in a room full of them.

A few things have stayed with me.

Sumaiya Balbale, one of the speakers, said that when you choose an industry or a company, you should prioritize talent density and company velocity. Surround yourself with high achievers and real expertise, at places that are growing fast or at least thinking about growth. That way you get to see the kinds of decisions made at every level of a company.

Nasser Chanda, on the panel about automation, AI and the future of work, talked about using AI as an individual, not only as a company: to multiply and complement your own skills and grow what you can contribute.

And Amjad Masad, the keynote speaker, told everyone to learn vibe coding and bring it into their businesses, whatever their size.

I'm hoping to put these lessons to work in my own ventures, in sha Allah.

## Who Will Thrive

*personal*

I have a hypothesis about what the people who do well in the coming years will have in common.

**Artists.** Since I started working, I've spent a lot of time around artists, and I think believing AI will replace them is a mistake. Artists see things and understand reality in ways the rest of us often can't. That's what will keep them sharp, and what lets them find the angle nobody else is looking at.

**Hungry people.** Information is everywhere now. What's rare is the person who goes out of their way to find resources and actually acts on them. I think the people who go to extraordinary lengths to learn, to make things, and to make knowledge their own, and who point that ambition toward good, will be the outliers. I think they'll be the happy ones too.

**People who ask a lot of questions.** New ideas have always come from curiosity. AI can't create something new on its own; it always needs a human to ask. The people who rely on themselves to learn, build and create will lead the way, for themselves and for everyone around them.

## The Decisive Moment

*building-briefly*

I've been thinking about Henri Cartier-Bresson, and about the idea that the best founders don't predict the future. They learn to see it coming.

Cartier-Bresson believed great photography had nothing to do with fast hands. It came from understanding a scene well enough to know what was about to happen. I think the same is true of starting companies. The founders who move fastest rarely win. The ones who see earliest do.

He said taking a photograph meant putting your head, your eye and your heart on the same axis. Not technical skill, not speed, but alignment. I keep coming back to that when I think about what separates founders who see opportunities from founders who only react to them.

Most people enter a market with just their eyes. They look at what already exists. The rarer move is to come in with all three: understanding (the head), observation (the eye), and real care about the problem (the heart). When those line up, you stop reacting to the market and start anticipating it.

Gaps show up before products do. Amazon, Airbnb, Clay: each was built by people who noticed the distance between two industries and decided to close it. The gap was visible to them before it was obvious to anyone else.

And what you study shapes what you're able to see. Cartier-Bresson trained his eye as a painter for years before he picked up a camera. A lot of founders try to spot opportunities without first doing the slow work of understanding the domains they're walking into.

For me, the founding moment was two industries colliding. When Avi and I looked at podcasting, we didn't see an audio problem. We saw a social media problem dressed up as a podcast. That framing became the whole thesis of Briefly.

## Caviar and White Chocolate

*building-briefly*

Caviar and white chocolate. On paper it sounds awful. At a Michelin-starred restaurant, it's a revelation.

Heston Blumenthal built a whole culinary philosophy around the idea that two ingredients taste good together when they share flavor compounds. It's called the food pairing hypothesis. Then researchers tested it across about 56,000 recipes and found something more interesting: it holds up in Western cooking, but some of the most celebrated dishes in East Asian cuisine, dishes refined over centuries, pair ingredients that share almost nothing chemically. The contrast is the point.

Opposites that complete each other. I think about this every time someone asks me how to pick a co-founder.

The usual advice is to find someone on your wavelength: shared vision, shared values, the same way of thinking. I think that's wrong, or at least incomplete. If you and your co-founder think the same way, you share the same blind spots. You'll make every bad decision twice as confidently. Every decision you agree on quickly is one neither of you stress-tested. You haven't reduced the risk. You've just made the conflict harder to see. Agreement isn't alignment; sometimes it's just a lack of friction.

At Briefly, everyone on the team is wired differently. We disagree all the time about how. We never disagree about why. That tension is a feature, not a bug. I've come to think the line that matters is mission alignment over method alignment.

Caviar and white chocolate works precisely because it sounds wrong. I think a team can be built the same way: look for the pairing that shouldn't make sense, until it does.

## Wrapping the New in the Familiar

*cognitive-science*

Humans are, quite literally, afraid of new food. It's called neophobia, a fear of the unfamiliar that our brains developed to keep us from eating something that might kill us.

Elisabeth Rozin spent years studying how cuisines around the world dealt with that fear, and the answer wasn't better food. It was better framing. Cultures developed their own signature flavor combinations of spices, sauces and bases, and used them to wrap unfamiliar ingredients in something the body already trusted. The new thing arrived inside the familiar thing, and people accepted it. Soy and miso. Olive oil and lemon. Tomato and cumin. Each one is a culture's shorthand for: this is safe, you've had this before, trust it.

I kept coming back to her work while thinking about how to launch Briefly, a product that does something genuinely new with podcast audio.

People don't reject new products because they're bad. They reject them because they're unfamiliar, and to the brain, unfamiliar reads as a threat. Your users aren't being irrational. They're being human. So the job isn't only to build something revolutionary. It's to build something revolutionary that feels like something they've always known.

Think about how often that has worked: new functionality inside a familiar container. The iPhone still looked like a phone. Airbnb looked like booking a hotel. Spotify looked like iTunes.

Every product needs its own version of a flavor combination: two or three familiar touchpoints that tell a new user, you already know how to use this. That's why I don't think the interface is where you innovate first. If what you're building is genuinely new, let the interface be the most boring part. Radical underneath, familiar on top. That isn't a compromise; it's the strategy.

At Briefly, we're not making the UI a spaceship. We're doing something new with podcast audio and wrapping it in a format people already trust, an interface that looks like something you've used a hundred times. That's on purpose. Comfort is a feature.

Find your familiar flavors first. Then let the innovation live inside them.
